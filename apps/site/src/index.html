Keep the woods working. Reconnect old-growth genetics in a dendritic pattern.

Dendritic Forest Management maps an old-growth spine of native habitat along the rivers, valleys and ridges of a working landscape, then checks every harvest plan against it. If a unit would break a link, the check names the unit and draws the break on the map before anyone starts a saw. Whether genes then flow along the spine is a question for field study, and the one we most want answered.

The old-growth spine

Rivers, valleys and ridges are a landscape's arteries. Dendritic Forest Management draws the forest along them as one branching spine, wider where the rivers are larger, and keeps it standing while the woods around it are worked. Left standing, the spine grows old.

  1. Map it whole

    Start from the water: streams set the branches, and larger rivers get wider corridors. Links over ridges and saddles close loops. The engine shows where one disturbance, such as a patch of blowdown or a new road crossing, would still cut a link, so the next loop goes where it is needed. The whole watershed is drawn before any one woodlot commits.

  2. Build it piece by piece

    Each woodlot that joins commits its stretch of the spine, and every later harvest plan is checked against it. The spine grows out from the first woodlot, north, south, east and west, toward the neighbor whose land would complete the next link.

  3. Let it grow old

    Retained forest keeps aging. With each stand's age on record, the spine can be projected forward: which links run through forest old enough to develop old-growth structure in 25, 50 or 100 years. Age is not condition; calling a stand old growth still takes field evidence.

  4. Plan for change

    As the climate warms, the conditions many species need move north and upslope. A spine that runs from warm valley floors to cool headwaters keeps a mapped route open toward cooler ground. The engine flags core areas with no linked route to a core cool enough for the warming you plan for.

Prairie, savanna and flat country

The same rules hold where the land is flat and the habitat is grass. A corridor is habitat at least the minimum width across, a road cuts it unless a crossing is recorded, and every number comes with its source. Old growth is not only forest: prairie and savanna that have never been plowed are old-growth grassland, and once plowed they recover slowly, if at all.

Stepping stones
Where habitat survives as patches, record the gap the species you plan for will cross, from a dispersal study. Patches within that gap count as linked. A gap never crosses a road without a recorded crossing.
Links on flat land
Swales, escarpments, moraines and shorelines carry links, and so do rights-of-way, field margins and hedgerows, each as wide as the land allows.
Never-plowed ground
A remnant recorded as never plowed or cleared counts at old-growth age from the start. Restored prairie waits for its age, like a replanted stand.
Fire and grazing
Prescribed burns, prescribed grazing, late-season mowing and brush management keep grassland open. With a recorded permission and reason they are upkeep, not a break. Plowing a patch is a break, and the check names the unit.
Beyond the map
On flat land, warming moves the conditions species need farther and faster than in hill country, so cooler ground usually lies past the edge of the landscape. An exit marks where the spine continues into the next landscape, and climate routes can end there.

Old growth of the place's own species

Only about a third of the world's forest is still primary forest, and nearly half of planted forest is plantation: one or two species, even-aged and in rows. Neither becomes old growth by standing long enough. The spine is meant to regrow old growth of each place's own trees and plants, so the engine records how habitat began and what it is made of.

Plantations
Record each stand's origin, with its source: naturally regenerating, planted, or plantation, as FAO defines them. A plantation still counts as a corridor today, but never counts at old-growth age in a projection, however old it gets.
Native share
Record the share of each stand's cover in native species, from a vegetation survey, and set the share your co-op requires. Habitat below it never counts at old-growth age, even a never-plowed prairie overrun by introduced grasses.
Native planting
A restoration planting inside a corridor can list its species, each with its native status from a source such as a state flora; your co-op can require the list. If one species is introduced, the planting is not permitted there, and the check names the unit and says why.

One question, asked before every harvest

Do the core areas stay linked by retained forest at least the minimum width, before and after the planned treatment units?

Pass links hold

Every core area linked today is still linked after the plan, and no unit enters a corridor without a recorded light-treatment permission.

Fail a link breaks

A unit cuts a corridor, narrows it below the minimum width, clears a core, or enters retained forest unpermitted. The check names each unit responsible and maps the lost corridor.

Incomplete inputs missing

A layer is missing, a geometry is wrong, or no source is recorded for the minimum width. The check never reports a pass it could not establish.

How the check works

  1. Map what is there. Core areas, retained corridors along streams and between stands, roads, open water and road crossings, each with its source.

  2. Set the minimum width. A site parameter in meters, with a recorded reason such as a co-op policy or a forester's recommendation.

  3. Remove what interrupts forest. Road surfaces and open water come out. A road keeps a corridor only where a crossing is recorded on it, and an assumed crossing is flagged for field checking.

  4. Draw in the plan. Each treatment unit comes out of the forest unless it is a recorded light treatment, such as single-tree selection with a stated reason.

  5. Test every link. Two cores are linked when a strip of forest the minimum width runs all the way between them. Links present today but missing after the plan fail the check.

Every result records the engine version and a checksum of its inputs, so the same inputs always give the same answer.

What a pass does and does not tell you

It establishes
That mapped retained habitat at least the minimum width across connects the same core areas after the plan as before, given the layers, crossings and any stepping-stone gap as recorded.
It does not establish
That wildlife moves through the corridor, that populations stay genetically viable, that a stand is old growth, or that a plan meets any regulation. Those need field evidence and qualified review.
Old growth
Mapping alone makes a stand an old-growth candidate. Calling it old growth requires field evidence of its structure, reviewed by someone other than the person who mapped it.
Time and climate
Projections use stand ages and join years as recorded and assume no fire, storm or new road; climate routes use the temperatures you supply. Neither predicts what species will do.
Distances
Horizontal, on a spherical Earth, within about half a percent. Slope and edge effects are not modeled.
